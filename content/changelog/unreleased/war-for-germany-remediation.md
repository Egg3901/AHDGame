## What changed

- **Your tail protects your troops, not everyone's.** The sheltering effect of a
  rear echelon was worked out for a whole side and applied to every soldier on it,
  so a coalition partner's supply train reduced your frontline casualties. In the
  War for Germany that was worth about a quarter off one side's entire line,
  because the Soviet air force spent the war parked behind it. Measured over 400
  battles, an ally's rear echelon used to cut your dead by 31%; it now cuts them
  by 8%, which is just the ordinary benefit of having an ally in the fight.
- **A war no longer decides itself before both sides arrive.** Defensive pacts
  answer immediately; joining a war of aggression takes a bill, a ratification and
  a mobilisation. That difference is real and stays. What was wrong is that the
  front did not wait: by the time Britain entered the war for Germany the line had
  already moved, and by Ireland's entry it had nearly finished moving. Ground now
  changes hands at 40% of the usual pace on the opening turn, reaching full pace
  fifty turns in.
- **An army stripped of its equipment can rearm.** State arsenals produce for the
  Soviet Union and East Germany every turn whatever happens, so their equipment can
  never reach zero. A market economy's only route to materiel is a defence contract,
  and the United States finished the war with nothing at all on its armoured and
  infantry divisions. Every other nation now gets one emergency lot into any store
  that has reached zero, which stops the moment the store is no longer empty. It is
  a floor, not a supply line: contracts remain the only way to actually equip an army.
- **Depth costs what it is worth.** A formation held out of the line bled slightly
  more per point of combat power than one standing in it. It now bleeds exactly the
  same, so holding a reserve is a decision about where your strength is, not a
  penalty for making it.

## What we got wrong in the post-mortem

The report said nothing capped what bleeds, and that past the frontage cap every
extra soldier took full casualties for no combat power. Measured, that is not what
the game was doing: a formation in depth already bled about a twelfth of one in the
line, against an engagement discount of a tenth. The real gap was 11%, not the
catastrophe described. The fix is correspondingly small.
