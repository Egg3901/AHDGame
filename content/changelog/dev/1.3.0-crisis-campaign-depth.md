## A crisis has a history

Each global confrontation now moves through posture, mobilization, operations,
settlement, and aftermath. Choices in one response window alter the stage and
conditions of the next. Countries also carry their own credibility, war
weariness, military commitment, humanitarian commitment, and covert exposure
from earlier decisions.

## Capacity shapes the menu

The most demanding responses require enough treasury capacity, military
readiness, logistics, domestic mandate, or intelligence confidence. The crisis
briefing explains which options are available and why others are out of reach.
These requirements are checked again when a government submits its response.

## Fog of war

Governments receive country-specific intelligence estimates instead of an exact
global risk score. Better intelligence and closer involvement produce tighter
estimates. Covert actions remain undisclosed to other governments unless the
operation is exposed.

## Consequences persist

Responses and aggregate outcomes now shape civilian strain, refugees,
infrastructure damage, arms proliferation, regional spillover, casualties, and
settlement momentum. These conditions continue changing between response
windows, influence campaign risk, and remain visible in the campaign briefing.
Escalatory outcomes also feed the shared Cold War tension system.

## A readable strategic board

The Conflicts page now explains the world-tension scale, its five bands, the
standing pressure floor, and every live contribution to that floor. Tooltips
describe how Vietnam, active crises, and nuclear stockpiles move the reading and
how the resulting DEFCON, procurement pressure, and détente penalty are derived.
An empty world no longer devotes most of the page to a blank theater map.

The new International Response Desk also puts every open global-response
campaign on the strategic board, even when its response card says National.
That label means each government owns its choice, not that the event is
domestic. Ordinary national crises such as industrial strikes remain on the
Crises and Actions pages.

The nuclear alert chain now requires two countries to field non-empty arsenals
with delivery capability. It no longer appears merely because the calendar has
reached 1957. Missing programmes now receive game-scaled, year-appropriate US,
Soviet or Russian, and UK baselines. Existing research, warheads, and production
orders are never replaced, and baseline production begins at zero.
