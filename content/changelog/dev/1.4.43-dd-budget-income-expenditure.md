Cut from development.

## Fixed

- Germany's sixteen states each received a federal transfer that the national
  budget booked as zero. The money showed up as state income with no matching
  national expense. It is now recorded on both sides.
- State budgets across Germany had frozen at the turn the two Germanies unified,
  because the process that maintains them was still looking for a country that
  no longer holds any territory. It now covers unified Germany as well, and
  reads that country's own tax laws rather than the ones the old West German
  book used, which had left every state's share of income tax and sales tax
  computing as nothing at all.
- Germany's finance minister could not direct the transfer between states. The
  allocation was looked up under the West German cabinet's name, which unified
  Germany does not use, so the pool always fell back to an even split per head.
- The national budget page showed no transfer at all for Germany's states. The
  lookup behind it fell back to the United Kingdom's figures for any country it
  did not explicitly name, reporting nothing rather than admitting it did not
  know.
- Revenue raised by legislation was read from a stored figure rather than being
  recalculated, so it stayed pinned to the size of the economy at the moment it
  was last written. Germany's had been set before unification and never moved.
- Berlin's budget held values that were not numbers. They spread silently
  through its revenue, balance and surplus, and survived every turn because a
  value that is not a number quietly fails every check rather than raising one.
  Broken figures are now rebuilt from the region's own output.

## Changed

- Tax bases can no longer outgrow the economy without limit. The pull holding
  them to the size of the country could be beaten outright by fast enough wage
  or trade growth, and once beaten there was no level it settled at. Bases may
  still outpace the economy when wages or trade genuinely run ahead, but only by
  a bounded margin, so the share settles instead of climbing forever.
- Non-tax revenue, which for a planned economy is the return the state takes
  from the enterprises it owns, was fixed at a figure written when the country
  was first drawn up. It is now held as a share of output, so it keeps pace with
  an economy that grows, splits or merges. East Germany's had been set against an
  economy a fifth of the size of the one it governs today.
- Germany is now treated as the centrally planned economy it is when the revenue
  ceiling is applied. It had been measured against the ceiling meant for market
  economies, which was taking roughly a quarter of its revenue every year.
