Cut from development.

## Fixed

- A central bank the government controls no longer sets its own policy rate. The
  autonomous technocrat chair ran on one test, whether the bank had a technocrat
  in the chair at all, and never asked whether the rate was the bank's to set. On
  a pre-1997 Bank of England it is not: the Chancellor sets Bank Rate, and the
  chair is a forecaster. So the chair kept moving the rate anyway, and since the
  government and the chair share a single rate-change cooldown, every autonomous
  move shut the Treasury's window the moment it reopened. The card read "on
  cooldown" in perpetuity and no minister could ever actually change the rate.
  The committee turn and the committee seeder both already made this check; the
  single-chair path was the one place it was missing.
- A bill that hands rate-setting to the government, or grants the bank its
  independence, can now be proposed. The provision had a modal, a validator and
  an enactment path, but no matching entry in the schema the request body is
  parsed against, so every bill carrying one was refused by the parser before any
  of that ran. Players saw an unexplained failure and read it as a broken bill.
- Such a bill can also now be proposed on its own. Returning rate-setting to the
  government is a complete statute, but the proposal form demanded a fully filled
  policy provision alongside it, and its one blank starter row could neither be
  removed nor be left blank. The bill was unsubmittable in both directions at
  once. A bill carrying a provision that stands on its own may now carry nothing
  else, and the same is true of a franchise or voter-registration bill.
- A government-controlled bank that still carries a dormant policy committee no
  longer refuses the government's rate change and sends it to a committee room
  the page hides for exactly those banks.
- A government-controlled bank no longer runs autonomous open-market operations.
  Monetary policy there belongs to the Treasury, for the same reason the rate
  does.

- A crisis response that promises to end the crisis sooner now does. Several
  options read "reduces duration by 2 turns" or similar and nothing anywhere
  implemented it: the only thing that could shorten a crisis was a funded
  collective aid response. A government that spent 1.5% of GDP on stimulus to
  end a recession sooner bought a cost and nothing else, then watched the
  recession run its full length, which read as a recession that would not
  resolve however it was answered. Stimulus, disaster relief and pandemic
  restrictions now cut the turns their copy advertises. The do-nothing choices
  still carry no reduction, and a crisis always runs at least one more turn.

## Changed

- Proposing a bank-independence or electoral-law statute now costs national
  political influence, on the same ladder every other provision uses. Those two
  were the only provisions that rode free, so the bill that moves monetary
  authority between the government and the bank, or rewrites who may vote, cost
  its sponsor nothing, while attaching an unrelated policy row to the very same
  statute cost the full ladder rate.
