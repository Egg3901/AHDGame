Cut from development. Raised on ticket #1134.

## Fixed

- A lot was priced against the size of the national economy, which is the
  intended design, but costed from a raw parts list that came to about a
  thousand. On the live world that meant a lot sold for 383,748,809 and
  consumed 1,091 of materials. Since a delivery pays a supplier the difference,
  99.9997% of every defence contract was profit. The defence budget was not
  buying equipment, it was being converted into one company's cash, and the
  minister awarding it could own part of that company.
- Building a lot now consumes 85% of what the lot sells for, in real steel,
  vehicles and electronics. The supplier keeps the remaining 15%, which is a
  normal profit for an arms manufacturer and a good business to be in. It is no
  longer a windfall.
- Because cost is now worked out from the price, the two can never drift apart
  again. This is what caused the problem: the price and the cost were measured
  in different things and ended up hundreds of thousands of times apart without
  anything flagging it.
- A hard ceiling stays on top of all of it. A lot can never be priced above
  what the economy says it is worth, and never above twice what it cost to
  build, whichever is lower.

## Changed

- Arsenals fill faster. A country's equipment budget used to buy about one lot
  per turn and now buys about one and three quarters. An infantry division's
  four lots take roughly two and a half turns instead of four, and equipping a
  national force from scratch takes about twenty-seven turns instead of a full
  game year. Procurement money still limits how fast an army can be re-equipped,
  which is the point of having a budget at all; it just limits it a little less
  tightly.
- A commodity shortage now squeezes the supplier's profit rather than raising
  the bill to the taxpayer. Prices are anchored to the economy and do not rise
  with the cost of steel, so a shortage is the manufacturer's problem to manage.

## Not changed

- Contracts already awarded are untouched, completely. They keep their agreed
  price AND the old profit split for every lot they have left to deliver. A
  player who signed an order under the old rules keeps those terms; a repair
  never takes away what someone already holds. The new economics apply only to
  orders awarded from here on.
- The limit on how much a country may put under contract in a window, and the
  per-turn payout limit added in 1.2.10, are both untouched.
- The emergency procurement freeze remains available as a switch.
