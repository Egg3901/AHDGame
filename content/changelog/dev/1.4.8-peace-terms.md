Cut from development.

## Fixed

- A system conversion now holds the election it schedules. When a regime
  collapsed into a new system, or a constitutional convention ratified one, the
  conversion recorded that a fresh election was due and then nothing acted on
  it. The government type changed, the ruling party lost its standing, and the
  same members stayed in the same seats until the ordinary cycle came round
  again. The record is now read on the turn it falls due, and the legislature is
  dissolved and returned to the country.

## Changed

- A dissolution can now reach any legislature. Calling an early election is
  normally reserved to parliamentary systems, because a presidential republic
  runs to fixed terms and a one party state has no reason to ask. A conversion
  forced on a country is not that kind of decision, so it is no longer held to
  that rule. Nothing else changes: a head of government still cannot call an
  early election where the constitution does not allow one.

- A forced dissolution does not spend the head of government's own allowance of
  early elections, and does not move the rest of the calendar. An early election
  called from inside the country shifts the following cycle, which is the price
  of calling it. One imposed from outside leaves every later election on the date
  it already had, and where that date now falls too close to run a nomination
  period, the affected election waits for the following cycle instead.
