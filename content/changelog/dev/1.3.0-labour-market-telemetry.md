## Labour supply and demand now get compared

A sector's headcount has always been sized from its revenue. Nothing has ever
checked that headcount against the number of people who live in the state.

The two halves were computed by systems with no edge between them. Civilian
labour force comes out of the metric engine, from working-age population and
participation. Sector headcount comes out of the corporation turn, from revenue.
Neither has ever read the other, so corporate employment has been unbounded by
population since world start.

The corporation turn now sums each state's desired headcount and writes two
readings to that state's macro metrics:

- **labourDemand**, the jobs the corporate sector wants there this turn
- **labourTightness**, that figure over the civilian labour force

A tightness of 1.0 means the corporate sector wants exactly the whole labour
force. Below 1 the market has slack. Above 1 it is oversubscribed, and the
excess is headcount no real person could fill.

The reading is not capped at the top of its range. On the current world some
states read in the hundreds, and the size of that overrun is the point of
measuring it.

States the metric engine has no labour force reading for record their demand and
no tightness, because unknown supply is not the same as infinite tightness.

## Rationing

Where a state is oversubscribed, sectors split the available workforce according
to the headcount they need and the wages they offer. A state at tightness 2.0
has half as many workers as its employers collectively want. A sector paying the
local average fills about half its jobs, while one paying more can win a larger
share and one paying less gets a smaller share.

The allocation is never first-come. Sectors are processed in an arbitrary
internal order, so processing order cannot hand one corporation a hidden
advantage. Paying every worker more also cannot create workers. Only paying more
than competing employers changes a sector's share.

Two numbers now appear on a sector: the workers it wants, and the workers it has.
In a normal state they are the same number.

## Capacity you cannot staff does not produce

The staffing shortfall now runs through the same output path as a strike. A
sector running at a quarter staff produces a quarter of its output, and earns
accordingly. Its plant, its capacity, and its books are untouched: it is
understaffed, not smaller, and it recovers the moment its state has the people.

Nobody loses a workforce overnight. Staffing moves at most ten percentage points
a turn, so a state that newly reads oversubscribed takes ten turns to reach the
full constraint, and a CEO watching output slide has time to divest, relocate,
or shrink capacity first. Recovery moves at the same rate, so a market that
frees up refills over the same window rather than snapping back.

Sectors in states with a normal labour market see no change whatsoever. The
factor is exactly 1 anywhere the market is not genuinely oversubscribed, and
exactly 1 for any state with no labour force reading, so missing data can never
throttle an economy.

## Plant jobs now use productive scale

The worker count originally existed only as a display number. It was inflated
100 times to make small early-game sectors show larger headcounts. Once workers
became a real production constraint, that display adjustment also made every
plant demand 100 times too many real people.

Plant employment now uses physical nameplate capacity at the original economic
productivity anchor. Commodity price spikes do not invent new jobs, and a large
late-game plant network can still outgrow its state without asking for an
impossible 100 million workers by default.

## The workforce responds to persistent vacancies

A shortage is no longer a permanent dead end. Sustained unfilled jobs gradually
raise labour participation by a bounded amount. They also attract working-age
residents from other states, and can strengthen permitted international
migration without overriding border policy or the world's migration limits.

These responses take multiple turns. New plant construction can still outrun
the available workforce, and wages remain the CEO's immediate lever while the
population catches up.

## The shortage is visible before and after building

Sector pages now show workers filled against workers wanted and explain that a
local shortage is limiting production. The build dialog warns when added
capacity will create jobs the current workforce cannot fill, and names the two
ways the constraint improves: raise relative pay or wait for the workforce to
grow.
