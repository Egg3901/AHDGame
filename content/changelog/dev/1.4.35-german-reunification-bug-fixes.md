Cut from development.

## Fixed

- A country converted to a one-party state by a settlement is now actually run as
  one. The rules that keep banned parties out of the legislature, and that stop a
  one-party government collapsing through ordinary coalition arithmetic, were
  reading the country's founding description of itself rather than what it had
  since become. A nation converted mid-game therefore had every one of those
  rules quietly switched off: banned parties could still propose and vote on
  legislation in a state where they were outlawed, and the machinery that is
  meant to hold a one-party government in place was not holding it.

- The leader who wins a reunification keeps their standing. A head of government
  carried into the unified state arrived with no record of their mandate, and the
  next confirmation would have reset them to a newcomer's confidence, erasing
  everything they had built. Their record now travels with them, and comes with a
  reunification dividend on top.

- The unified legislature belongs to the side that won it. Reunification used to
  ban every party except the ruling one, which dissolved the winning side's own
  coalition partners at the moment they won, and then left the defeated side's
  parties sitting on most of the benches of a state where they were illegal. The
  bloc that crossed over is now tolerated rather than banned, and the parties that
  are outlawed give up the seats they held.

- The winning cabinet governs the unified country. Every minister of the
  victorious state used to be dismissed while the defeated state's ministers
  stayed in office. Portfolios that exist on both sides now carry across to their
  counterparts, those with no counterpart end with the state that had them, and
  the defeated cabinet stands down.

- Reunification no longer shrinks the country's borrowing power. The unified
  treasury took on the absorbed state's debt ceiling in place of its own, which
  could leave a far larger country with a far smaller limit than it had before.
  The two ceilings now add, in the same way the treasuries and the bond books
  already did.

- Offices no longer point at places and posts that stopped existing. The outgoing
  head of government went on reading as head of government after handing the
  office over, the incoming one arrived still holding the title of a country that
  had just been dissolved, and anyone seated in a region absorbed by another kept
  naming the region that had gone. All three were stored rather than worked out
  when read, so they showed a title nobody held and were read that way by
  elections and by the office rankings.

- A war settlement that changes a country's system of government now says which
  system in plain words. The news wire had been printing the internal name for
  it.

## Added

- A settlement that converts a country to a one-party state can name which party
  takes power. Left unnamed, the strongest party in the country takes it, which on
  a settlement imposed by force is usually the government the winner just fought.
  The choice is on both the victor's terms window and a negotiated offer, and an
  offer that names a party says so plainly to the country being asked to accept
  it. The war record and the news wire name the party that took power too, and
  say that every other party was banned, rather than reporting a settlement of
  that weight as an ordinary change of government.
