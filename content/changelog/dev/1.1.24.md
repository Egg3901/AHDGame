## Producer cost pass-through

Commodity prices came from supply/demand pressure alone: nothing connected
what a producer pays for inputs to what its output sells for. On production
that produced a sector-class failure: fertilizers at 2.26x base, plastics
1.60x, freight 1.61x — while food, whose recipe spends 57% of nameplate
revenue on those inputs, sold into a global glut at 0.58x. 92 of 149
agriculture sectors worldwide ran negative engine margins, with no action a
player could take to price their way out.

Each commodity's effective base price is now lifted by a damped share of its
producers' input-cost excess before supply/demand pressure applies:
`min(1.75, 1 + 0.5 x (input cost index − 1))`, floored at 1. Cheap inputs
never discount below the market engine's own price — gluts still clear; only
cost squeezes transmit. Ratios are the prior turn's (the same lag the input
bill itself uses), so there is no same-turn circularity, and the 0.5 damping
makes chained recipes converge instead of compound. This also gives every
input shortage a self-correcting path: dear inputs raise output prices, which
restores producer margins, which attracts supply.

## The exchange prices the policy trend

The share-price formula discounted earnings by the spot prime rate. With
hourly turns and an active FOMC, spot moved ±0.75pp every few turns (16
changes in six days on production, 3.0% → 6.9% → 3.5% → 5.0%), and full
instant transmission whipsawed every listed corporation's valuation with it.

`fomcMeetingTurn` now advances a per-bank EMA (`primeRateSmoothed`, ~6-turn
window, every bank every turn including dormant ones), and the share-price
formula discounts by that. Everything that genuinely reprices immediately —
loans, coupons, growth costs — stays on spot. FOMC gameplay is untouched: the
cadence is six meetings a game-year, which is the realistic rate; only the
market's transmission was wrong.
