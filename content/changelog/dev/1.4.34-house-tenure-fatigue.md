Cut from development.

## Fixed

- Long service used to subtract a flat charge from a representative's political
  influence and favourability, one that grew with every term and stopped only at
  a total wipe. Because it was a fixed charge against standings that are
  themselves modest, it fell hardest on ordinary members and barely touched the
  most prominent ones: a representative of average influence had none left after
  five terms, and none of their favourability either. Time in office now takes a
  small share of both, so every officeholder keeps the same proportion of their
  standing however long they have served, and no length of service can reduce
  anyone to nothing.

- In the 1962 United States House the old charge had reached the point of
  erasure. Both returning nominees in Florida had contested every prior cycle,
  which left them with no influence at all and barely half their favourability,
  and a first-time nominee for a party holding under two percent of the state's
  registered voters was carrying 88 percent of the vote and every one of the
  eight seats. Forty-one of the fifty-five open House races were running under
  the same charge, and delegations across the country were being handed to
  brand-new parties on the strength of it. Those races now turn on organisation,
  registration and the candidates themselves, as they were meant to.

## Changed

- Time in office is now a modest tilt against entrenchment rather than a term
  limit in disguise. A representative at the maximum still carries 85 percent of
  their influence and favourability into the race, and a first-term member is
  untouched, exactly as before. Long-serving incumbents will hold their seats
  more often than they did last week; they are no longer being removed by the
  vote engine rather than by voters.
