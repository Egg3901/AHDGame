Cut from development.

## Changed

- A cabinet office page is now readable only by the minister seated in it, by the
  head of government and head of state of that same country, and by admins.
  Anybody else sees the office letterhead and a notice naming who may look inside.
- The notice names the real offices, so a visitor to the United States defence
  page is told that the seated Secretary of Defense and the President of the
  United States may view it, rather than being shown a bare refusal.
- The head of government and head of state read an office without gaining its
  levers. Every control still belongs to the minister who holds the seat.
- A reigning monarch counts as a head of state. A crown is not an elected office,
  so the British monarch and the Japanese emperor are recognised by the throne
  they sit on rather than by a seat in the rolls, and keep the access their
  office implies.
- The seat, the department and the name of the minister holding it stay visible.
  Those are already published on the cabinet list and on the minister's own page,
  and hiding them here would only make the page look broken.

## Fixed

- Foreign governments could read another country's defence page in full: combat
  power, active personnel, available manpower, average readiness, appropriation
  and force upkeep, along with the military, arsenal, nuclear, budget and
  operations tabs. None of it was reaching the browser by accident; the endpoint
  behind the page served it to anyone who knew the address.
- The withheld figures are no longer sent at all, rather than being hidden after
  they arrive, so they cannot be recovered from the page.
- How many ministerial actions a minister has left is no longer published outside
  the office.
- The nuclear programme console follows the same rule as the office around it. It
  previously refused everyone but the seated minister, which would have shown a
  head of government an error inside a page they are entitled to read.
