Cut from development.

## Changed

- The Issue Bond card now says what borrowing actually costs. It states that the
  corporation receives the whole face value in cash at issuance, pays a coupon
  every turn, and then repays the whole face value in one payment on the maturity
  turn rather than gradually, and that both the coupon and that repayment are
  charged on every unit issued, including units nobody bought.

- The projected impact panel now shows the principal due at maturity and the
  turn it comes due, beside the interest cost it used to show on its own. The
  expensive half of the debt was the half that was never quoted.

- Issuing now carries a plain warning that liquid capital has to stay positive.
  A coupon or a repayment that drives it below zero, on a corporation whose
  assets cannot cover its debt, is what a default is, and the consequences of one
  are stated at the point of borrowing: holders marked down to ten cents on the
  dollar, and the credit score pinned to CCC for 96 turns.

- A CEO now sees a standing notice on every tab of their corporation page, above
  the tabs, naming the next bond principal falling due, its amount, and the turn
  it lands on. It turns a warning once the repayment is within 24 turns, and says
  so outright when liquid capital is already below the amount owed. The repayment
  was only ever visible on a tab there was no reason to open between borrowing
  the money and being charged for it.

- The Outstanding Bonds table now says that the face value in its column is
  repaid in full, out of liquid capital, on the maturity turn.

- The bonds guide now describes issuance from the borrower's side, and its
  section on defaults now names maturity as a trigger rather than describing
  coupons alone.

## Fixed

- The outstanding debt figure on the Bonds tab was converted into the display
  currency twice for any corporation that does not keep its books in the anchor
  currency, overstating what it owed.

- Bond face values on the Bonds tab were labelled with the corporation's current
  currency rather than the one the bond was issued in. Moving a corporation to
  another country re-denominates the company but leaves its outstanding bonds
  where they were, so any bond predating a move was shown as an amount in a
  currency it is not held in. Each bond is now shown in its own currency.
