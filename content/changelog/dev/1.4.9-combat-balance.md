Cut from development.

## Fixed

- A front now holds only so much in contact at once. Formations beyond its
  width are held in depth, where they fight at a tenth of their weight and
  take about a ninth of the casualties a formation in the line takes. (This
  entry originally said depth does not fight and does not bleed at all. It
  does both, at a heavy discount; corrected 2026-09-06.) Before
  this a side's casualties grew with the size of the force it committed
  almost regardless of the enemy, so sending more divisions cost more men
  even when they could not all reach the fighting. The winning side routinely
  lost far more men than the side it was beating. A joint offensive now
  trades about two dead for each one it inflicts, against eleven before.

- Armour and safe roles no longer leave a formation more exhausted. Readiness
  after a battle was worked out in a way that inverted its own terms, so an
  armoured division ended a fight more spent than the infantry beside it, and
  a carrier that lost three men ended more spent than either.

- Breaking off is exceptional again. It was happening in about nine battles
  out of ten, which made the lighter casualties and the cheaper ground that
  come with it a permanent discount rather than the consequence of a decision.
  It now happens in roughly one battle in three.

## Changed

- Pressing an attack turn after turn now wears an army down much faster than
  attacking at a measured pace. A rested formation pays an ordinary price for
  a battle; one that has been fighting without rest pays up to four times as
  much for the same engagement. Continuous pace leaves little room for rest,
  and there is now a real choice between attacking again and letting the line
  recover.

- Because a battle no longer empties a formation, the readiness a posture
  buys is felt during a war rather than only before it. An army held at high
  alert is meaningfully steadier in a long campaign than one left in garrison.

- The role you assign a formation now decides whether it stands in the line
  when a front is crowded. Frontline and flank units take their places first,
  then support, then anything held back.
