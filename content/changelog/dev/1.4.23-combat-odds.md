Cut from development.

## Fixed

- The odds shown beside an offensive are now the chance of winning it. They
  were a measure of relative strength, and the battle that followed was
  decided almost entirely by that strength: a projected 16 percent won about
  one battle in ten thousand, a projected 35 percent won one in twenty, and
  anything above about 60 percent won every time. The war room and the field
  manual both called the number a chance, so a commander who read it the way
  they were told to saw an enemy winning fights they should have lost. A
  battle is now fought at the projected odds plus the fortune of the day, and
  the share of battles a given projection wins now matches the projection
  itself: within a point measured over the resolver alone, and within about
  three on a replay of the live German front, which is inside the sampling
  error of that replay.

- Underdogs can win, and overwhelming force is no longer a guarantee. The
  projection has always been capped at 2 percent and 98 percent, which only
  ever meant anything if it was a probability. Now it is one. A hopeless
  attack is still hopeless and an overwhelming one still wins nearly always,
  but neither is certain, and a front can turn.

- The "They attack" row counted the wrong army. It projected the enemy's
  offensive against your side's attack roster, which is you plus whichever
  allies had filed an offensive of their own. Defending is not something you
  file for: every nation with troops at the front stands in the line when the
  enemy moves. So allies who were holding the ground beside you were left out
  of the estimate, and the row read far higher than the battle that would
  actually be fought. It now counts everyone who is there.

- The field manual described the "They attack" row as your chance when the
  enemy attacks. It is theirs. Each row is the chance of whoever is attacking
  in that row, which is also why the two never add to 100.

## Changed

- Results are more varied. Because luck now moves a battle, individual
  outcomes swing wider. On a replay of the live German front, grinding
  stalemate gave way to decided results: costly defeats fell from about seven
  battles in ten to two, while decisive victories, clear victories and routs
  all became common. Over a campaign the stronger side still wins as often as
  its strength says it should.

- Breaking off is more common, and the fighting is slightly less costly for
  it. On the same replay a side broke off in about four battles in ten before
  and about seven in ten now, and average losses fell by roughly a tenth on
  both sides. A wider spread of outcomes means more battles reach the point
  where one side has clearly had enough.

- Casualties follow the battle that was actually fought rather than the
  strength comparison it started from. A side that had a good day is not
  billed for bleeding its own luck spared it.
