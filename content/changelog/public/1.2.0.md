## Specializations

Finish either branch of a decade's lane and a new tier opens: three
specializations, each with its own capstone technology.

- **Scale** cuts costs: cheaper inputs, lower wages, cheaper growth.
- **Premium** raises what your output sells for and strengthens your brand.
- **Resilience** shields you from dominance penalties and tariffs and makes
  expansion cheaper.

You can take exactly one per decade. The other two lock, and switching means
abandoning the decade, so a corporation now has a build, not a checklist.
Across the two lanes and three specializations there are six distinct ways
to play every decade in every industry: 1,020 new technologies in total.

## Effects that do something

The new tier leans on effects wired straight into the plants economy: a new
realised-price bonus, input cost cuts, output increases, labor savings,
expansion discounts, and penalty shields. This builds on the margin fix in
1.1.8.

## Pacing

Specializations cost double and capstones triple a decade's base research
cost, so a well-funded corporation now finishes its chosen path deep into the
decade rather than in the first year or two. Idle research points late in a
decade should be rare.

## Your progress is safe

- Everything you have researched stays researched, at the price you paid.
- Past decades still grant their baseline technologies free to new
  corporations, but not the specializations. Those were choices, and history
  does not make choices for you.
- Computer-run corporations pick specializations under the same rules you do.

## The Vietnam War

Between 1955 and 1975 a war in Vietnam now runs as a chain of crises rather
than a single event. It starts small, with advisors and training missions,
and climbs a ladder of six rungs: advisors, materiel and money, an incident
in the Gulf of Tonkin, an air campaign, a ground commitment, and a long war
with no end date.

Every rung puts the same decision to both superpowers at once. The American
president and the Soviet premier each answer for their own country, and
neither one's choice closes the other's. You can deepen the commitment, hold
where you are, or pull back.

- **Deepening it costs money.** A pledge is priced as a share of your GDP and
  paid out of the treasury, and the share grows at every rung.
- **Deepening it costs approval** with the part of your country that does not
  want the war, and that cost grows the longer the war has already run. The
  third year of a ground war is far more expensive to prolong than the first
  month of an advisory mission.
- **Pulling back costs approval** with hawks, and costs more the higher the
  rung you are climbing down from.
- **One capital's restraint does not cancel the other's war.** Standing down
  drains what you have committed before it brings the rung itself down.

The rung the war sits on is not decoration. It sets global readiness, so an
escalating Vietnam moves DEFCON and, through it, the detente board's
stand-down gate and the whole home front calculation. It moves both blocs'
cohesion, which is what a bloc can actually supply to a proxy war and how
hard its threats land in a confrontation. It creates war weariness at home
that gets worse the longer the fighting runs, and it raises defence
procurement demand.

When a rung's crisis ends, what follows is decided by where the two of you
left the ladder. A war both sides kept feeding climbs. A war both sides
talked down ends, and does not restart.
