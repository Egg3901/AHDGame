## Specializations

Finish either branch of a decade's lane and a new tier opens: three
specializations, each with its own capstone technology.

- **Scale** cuts costs: cheaper inputs, lower wages, cheaper growth.
- **Premium** raises what your output sells for and strengthens your brand.
- **Resilience** shields you from dominance penalties and tariffs and makes
  expansion cheaper.

You can take exactly one per decade. The other two lock, and switching means
abandoning the decade, so a corporation now has a build, not a checklist.
Across the two lanes and three specializations there are six distinct ways
to play every decade in every industry: 1,020 new technologies in total.

## Effects that do something

The new tier leans on effects wired straight into the plants economy: a new
realised-price bonus, input cost cuts, output increases, labor savings,
expansion discounts, and penalty shields. This builds on the margin fix in
1.1.8.

## Pacing

Specializations cost double and capstones triple a decade's base research
cost, so a well-funded corporation now finishes its chosen path deep into the
decade rather than in the first year or two. Idle research points late in a
decade should be rare.

## Your progress is safe

- Everything you have researched stays researched, at the price you paid.
- Past decades still grant their baseline technologies free to new
  corporations, but not the specializations. Those were choices, and history
  does not make choices for you.
- Computer-run corporations pick specializations under the same rules you do.

## The Vietnam War

Between 1955 and 1975 a war in Vietnam now runs as a chain of crises rather
than a single event. It starts small, with advisors and training missions,
and climbs a ladder of six rungs: advisors, materiel and money, an incident
in the Gulf of Tonkin, an air campaign, a ground commitment, and a long war
with no end date.

Every rung puts the same decision to both superpowers at once. The American
president and the Soviet premier each answer for their own country, and
neither one's choice closes the other's. You can deepen the commitment, hold
where you are, or pull back.

- **Deepening it costs money.** A pledge is priced as a share of your GDP and
  paid out of the treasury, and the share grows at every rung.
- **Deepening it costs approval** with the part of your country that does not
  want the war, and that cost grows the longer the war has already run. The
  third year of a ground war is far more expensive to prolong than the first
  month of an advisory mission.
- **Pulling back costs approval** with hawks, and costs more the higher the
  rung you are climbing down from.
- **One capital's restraint does not cancel the other's war.** Standing down
  drains what you have committed before it brings the rung itself down.

The rung the war sits on is not decoration. It sets global readiness, so an
escalating Vietnam moves DEFCON and, through it, the detente board's
stand-down gate and the whole home front calculation. It moves both blocs'
cohesion, which is what a bloc can actually supply to a proxy war and how
hard its threats land in a confrontation. It creates war weariness at home
that gets worse the longer the fighting runs, and it raises defence
procurement demand.

When a rung's crisis ends, what follows is decided by where the two of you
left the ladder. A war both sides kept feeding climbs. A war both sides
talked down ends, and does not restart.

## Defence contracts are real obligations now

Awarding a procurement contract commits its whole cost against your defence
appropriation the moment you sign it. You can never have more contracts open
than your budget can cover, and an order that would go past that is refused
with the figure you actually have left. Deliveries draw that commitment down;
cancelling an order hands it straight back.

Building materiel now costs the supplier something, so a contract pays a
company its margin rather than handing over the whole price as profit.

You set the price per lot yourself, inside a band running from what the plant
spends to build a lot up to the going market rate, and you choose the grade you
are buying: cheap mass or premium kit. Leave both blank for a fair price at
whatever quality the supplier can build.

On the other side of the table, a contractor decides how many of a plant's four
production lines work each order. More lines deliver faster. The price per lot
does not change.

Nothing is thrown away quietly any more. When an order does not move, both
order books say why: part of a lot built, budget short this turn, more built
than the order still needs. You can see lots ordered, built and waiting,
delivered, paid, and still committed, on every contract.

And if a defence minister awards a contract to a company they own or hold a
serious stake in, it is disclosed on the contract, reported on the wire, and it
costs them with the public.

## The Vietnam War reaches the map

The escalation ladder now creates the war it describes. When the two
superpowers push it to the air campaign, a real conflict opens in Vietnam:
the Republic of Vietnam backed by the West against the Democratic Republic
of Vietnam backed by the East, fought over southern soil, with the front
starting on the parallel. It appears on the conflicts map, has its own
record page, and runs through the same battle system as every other war.

The front follows the ladder. Climb higher and it intensifies. Step back
below the air campaign and it winds down. Talk the ladder all the way to
nothing and the war ends as a stalemate, with neither side winning, and it
does not come back.

Neither superpower is a belligerent. They are patrons, and they stay
patrons: a crisis decision cannot quietly put your country at war with the
other superpower. What you commit on the ladder is what reaches the front.

## Cold War dials come from the server

The Cold War command console kept readiness, bloc cohesion and everything
derived from them in your browser, which meant two players looking at the
same war could see different numbers. The console now reads them from the
game. Open any Cold War page and the dials load from the world's actual
state, so DEFCON is DEFCON for everyone.

## Vietnam starts with a decision

The war no longer just appears. When the chain starts, both administrations
get a crisis of their own with twenty four hours on the clock, and the two
read nothing alike. Washington is asked whether to commit the United States,
hold to the advisory mission it inherited, or start getting out. Moscow is
asked whether to back Hanoi in full, hold the present level of assistance,
or step back and let Peking carry it.

The clock is real time, not turns, and it is on the decision where you make
it. Miss it and your government holds to the advisory mission: the war
simmers where it is and comes back around rather than stalling or dragging
you into something a timer chose for you.

What you pick moves the ladder. Committing pushes toward materiel and
everything past it. Stepping back drains what you have committed and can
bring the whole thing down a rung.

## The war gets covered

Every rung and every decision is now reported. The world reads one story,
Washington reads another and Moscow reads a third, written for the desk and
the year rather than the player. Advisers arrive and nobody calls it a war.
Shipments outgrow anything anyone can call training aid. A destroyer reports
an attack in the gulf and the second report is thinner than the first.
Bombing starts. Divisions land. The casualty lists lengthen and the
appropriations keep passing.

The coverage runs to the in-game news feed, the in-game wire, and the
Discord news and national channels, all at once.


## Country pages tell you where to go

A country page is now a directory into everything that country contains.
The header is shorter, and under it is a grouped list of every place you can
go: Politics, Government, Economy and Nation.

Each entry carries a live number, so the list answers "is anything happening"
as well as "where do I go". Elections shows how many races are live. The
budget shows the balance as a share of GDP. The legislature shows bills in
front of it. The central bank shows the prime rate. The economy shows GDP.

Entries that do not apply to your country are not there at all. The Supreme
Court is on the United States page. The Command Economy dashboard is on
planned economies. Referendums appear once your country has run one. If the
Cold War is running and you play Washington or Moscow, there is a Cold War
entry with the current DEFCON on it.

Everything that was on the page before is still on the page, further down.
The whole thing is built for a phone: one column, and every row is big enough
to tap.
