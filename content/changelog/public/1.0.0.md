This is the 1.0 milestone. It opens the 1953 era: a world where the map, the
money, the armies and the voters all belong to that year. Around it sit the
largest systems we have ever shipped at once: a Cold War you play as a
board, conflicts that live at real places on the map, and an electorate built
cell by cell from real census data. At the end there is a preview of the next
big one, a ground-up rework of how corporations produce and sell, which we
want you to see before it goes live.

## A founding election for 1953

When a 1953 world begins, the game runs a real founding election before anyone
takes office. Parties campaign, voters vote, and the winners govern. Every
participating country starts from a mandate its voters actually gave it, and
your first days in a new world are election days. Later era starts keep their
seeded opening governments, so their first government still matches the year
selected.

That matters more than it sounds. The strength of every government you will
deal with, ally with, or campaign against traces back to a result you can go
and look at.

## An electorate built from people

Under every election in a 1953 or 1991 world is a new engine. Each state's
electorate is assembled from its census: occupation, age, education, religion
and urbanization, crossed into thousands of population cells, each with its
own positions and its own turnout. The cells are fitted so the totals match
what the census counted.

Everything downstream reads those cells:

- **A state leans the way it does because of who lives there.** Leans are
  derived from the population, not assigned. A mill town and a farm county
  pull differently because their people do.
- **Projections aggregate cells, so they behave.** The US House is projected
  district by district rather than by a national swing, seat forecasts line up
  with how the map breaks, and no world can report more votes than it has
  eligible voters.
- **Campaigns meet groups, not averages.** Appeal to a position and the cells
  that share it respond; the ones that do not, do not.
- **It moves with the year.** Voting age, the shape of the population and how
  groups drift all track the live calendar, and every ten years a census
  redraws House seats and electoral votes.

New worlds run this engine from turn one. Worlds seeded before 1.0 keep their
original panels until their next reset.

## The world follows the clock

A world's start is anchored to its era. Its behaviour is not. The rule the
whole program was built on is **gravity, not rails**: history pulls, it never
steers, and the calendar can never overrule your policy.

- **Crises know what decade it is.** Cyber, tech-bubble and disinformation
  crises arrive when a world's live year reaches them, whatever year it was
  seeded in. Pandemics can strike any era; the 1957 Asian flu killed over a
  million people.
- **Prices and exchange rates start where the era started**, then move on
  their own.
- **Statehood is a live process.** Territories build pressure inside era
  windows and are admitted with their seats, offices and elections attached.
- **Copy is checked against the decade.** Legislation text and player events
  that are wrong for their year fail a lint before they ship.

## The Cold War is a board you play on

The largest single piece of work in the release. Alignment is a system with a
ledger, a price, an opponent, and consequences.

### Where everyone stands

Every nation carries a share for each bloc plus an uncommitted remainder,
always totalling 100, seeded from an authored table covering 199 entities,
colonies and protectorates included. The **Cold War Ledger** under World lists
every nation's shares, lead, trend and band.

Sweden and Yugoslavia both look neutral at a glance. Sweden is genuinely
undecided; Yugoslavia is being pulled hard in two directions. A single
left-or-right number cannot tell you that. The ledger can.

Which poles exist depends on the live year. 1953 is West against East, the
Non-Aligned Movement becomes a pole in 1961, and from 1991 the board is
Washington, Moscow, Beijing and non-aligned. The threshold to join scales with
how many poles are in play, because a four-way split cannot open a fifty-point
lead. Colonies inherit their ruler's alignment and drop to a residual when
they gain independence.

### Drifting, and changing sides

Nations drift as the game runs, and drift is slow and stubborn. A country deep
in one camp barely moves. An uncommitted country is the hardest of all to
shift, which is what makes the neutrals worth fighting over. There is a
deliberate deadband in the middle where nobody owns a country.

Drift far enough and a nation changes sides for real. Membership follows where
a country's sympathies actually went, not who spent money on it most recently.
Patronage buys ties and obligations, not loyalty.

Limits, because a system that moves nations around needs them:

- **Non-player nations act on their own.** They join and leave under their own
  judgement.
- **Players are never moved for.** A request to a player-run nation asks; it
  does not act.
- **Alignment governs only the blocs you join by picking a side.**
  Organisations without an alignment channel, the Commonwealth among them, are
  untouched.
- **A bloc's founders can never be expelled.**
- **Non-alignment is the remainder, not a pole.** A non-aligned nation has no
  patron at all. That is the whole point of the movement, which forms in 1961
  and can be watched coming into existence mid-run in a 1953 game.

### Blocs are not ordinary alliances

NATO and the Warsaw Pact seat the alliances they actually had, and in a Cold
War world they are **blocs**: a defence pact that also pays and squeezes the
countries in its sphere.

### Influence is a lever you pay for

Every organisation has an **Influence** tab. A member's foreign minister can
spend the bloc's shared fund to pull a nation their way.

The price is a share of the target's own economy, so a hundred million dollars
goes much further in a small country than a large one, and no single push can
do everything however much you spend. The tab quotes the **delivered** price,
not a list price, and refuses a locked target before taking your money.

Nothing answers you automatically. There is no free counter-move: your rival
takes ground back only by spending on it too. Opposing pushes cancel each
other before the ceiling is applied, so a contested country resolves
proportionally. A country nobody is contesting is cheap; a bidding war over
one you both want is expensive.

The tab opens as a situation room. You see how the world splits between blocs,
by economy or by country count, then every nation you could court ranked by
the question you are actually asking: where is my money cheapest, who is
closest to joining me, where is there a crisis, and which of my own members is
weakest. Pick one and you get a dossier: where it stands, what is pulling on
it, what it costs, and what your rival did to it last turn. You are told which
bloc moved a country and by how much, never how much they paid.

A **crisis desk** lifts a ceiling when a country is in trouble. It lifts the
ceiling; it grants nothing. You still have to spend.

Positions track to a tenth of a point, so a modest sustained effort adds up
over a year. The alignment screens call countries by the name they had in
their era.

**Aid** pays a member's treasury and buys loyalty at the same time, but only
reaches countries already in your bloc, so it holds what you have rather than
taking more. **Sanctions** cost the sanctioner: squeeze a country and your own
standing there falls while the embargo lasts. That is a good way to lose a
client, and a good trap to walk a rival into.

## War is a place on the map

A conflict is a real object: a host country, a region, two sides, and a type,
an interstate war, an intervention, a civil war, or a war of independence. Its
terrain sets both the combat maths and the kind of enemy you meet, so a jungle
war and a war on the plains do not play the same. Armor rolls well across a
developed plain and poorly through high passes; amphibious and recon units
come into their own in littoral and jungle; open sea belongs to hulls.

Either side can be backed by a bloc, which is what turns a local fight into a
proxy war; a conflict nobody backs stays local. Units deploy to a conflict
rather than to a fixed front, and sit in the homeland reserve when there is
nowhere to be. Doctrine and trait trees shape what your forces are good at.
The live Situation Board allocates combat power across those fronts, and
Combat Command issues the orders. The conflicts hub shows every live conflict
in the world, on the map and in a list, with casualties by theater. The Cold
War Ledger carries the wider alignment contest.

**Who you can attack is read off the war itself.** A conflict knows who is
fighting on each side, so the command panel offers exactly the nations across
the front you have selected. Alliance membership is read from the actual
alliance rolls: the Warsaw Pact roll is the East, NATO's is the West, and a
nation in neither pact is non-aligned in fact. In 1953 that includes West
Germany, which does not join NATO until 1955. Neutral armies massing read as
neutral, not as either side moving.

### Armies you have to pay for

- **Eighteen more countries have real armed forces**, each with branches that
  appear and retire on their actual dates. East Germany's NVA runs 1956 to
  1990; the Austrian Bundesheer starts in 1955; Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia
  retire with the states themselves.
- **Authored orders of battle.** What a country fields is what it plausibly
  fielded, and every roster is calibrated to start inside its own upkeep
  budget.
- **Recruiting costs real resources.** A new unit spends a ministerial action,
  national manpower, and treasury, priced as a share of your own country's
  output.
- **Manpower is the hard block.** A unit is raised at full strength and costs
  full strength from the national pool. Disbanding returns the men but not the
  money, so churn stays expensive.
- **Modernising costs money up front**, priced off what the unit would cost to
  build new and rising as you approach cutting edge. Modern kit also costs
  more to maintain, deliberately more than the fighting power it adds: a
  cutting-edge division beats an old one and loses to two of them. Keeping
  cheap older equipment is a real answer for an army that needs numbers. The
  button shows both figures before you press it, the price today and the new
  upkeep.
- Spending past your surplus is national debt, not an error. The panel shows
  the split.

### Generals

You do not promote a general. They promote themselves by fighting. Experience
comes from battles they personally led, so a general who is posted, given
units, and sent into an offensive climbs on their own. The corps roster names
each officer's rank, specialisation and progress to the next promotion, and
their profile is where skill points are spent. Field Marshal is the top of the
ladder.

## Elections are counted from the map up

- **District-by-district House projections.** Seat forecasts line up with how
  the map actually breaks, not with a national swing.
- **Totals reconcile.** A world can never report more votes than it has
  eligible voters, and crowded races add up correctly.
- **Every race gets a challenger.** Wherever the other major party has a real
  presence, it fields a candidate for Governor and Senate.
- **Presidential primaries run on one engine**, and their percentages
  reconcile to 100.
- **Incumbency and fatigue are real.** A sitting leader's advantage scales
  with their approval, and long-tenured parties pick up a fatigue penalty.
- **Running mates and endorsements.** Presidential races outside the US can
  pick a running mate, and a suspended candidate can still be chosen as one.
- **The race page was rebuilt**, with separate primary and general modes.
- **More maps**, including county-level results and a Soviet Union 1953 map.

## The opposition thinks

Non-player politicians got a new brain, with rails.

- **Context-aware scoring.** They weigh a decision by the situation in front
  of them rather than by a fixed table.
- **Unpredictability that is not noise.** Harder to read without being random.
- **Smarter economic sweeps**, so the AI reads the economy before acting on
  it.
- **Player-impact rails.** There is an explicit budget for how much the AI may
  disrupt any single player, because a smarter opponent that hounds one person
  is a worse game.
- **Stance drift.** Every few turns a non-player politician nudges its
  economic and social positions toward the lean of the state it represents,
  stepped by how stubborn it is: fully mobile at one end, immovable at the
  other.

## Politicians who sound and look like where they are from

Name generation now draws every country from a pool of its own.

- **Six new name pools** (France, Italy, Spain, Sweden, Turkey, Russia),
  written to serve 1953 and 2019 alike. East Germany draws on the German pool,
  Scotland and Wales on the UK pool.
- **Conventions, not just tokens.** Spanish names take a second surname much
  of the time. Russian surnames inflect for gender, which is the difference
  between a plausible Duma and machine output. The share of women follows each
  real legislature.
- **Portraits cover 17 countries**, up from 2, with around a thousand faces,
  so a Japanese or Nigerian politician gets a matching face. Faces are sourced
  deliberately from the anonymous middle of a legislature: people who actually
  sat, never led a country or party, and are barely covered outside their own
  language. Fame is the thing property-based rules cannot exclude, so
  obscurity is the filter.
- The character creator draws your randomised name from your rolled country
  too.

## The economy in 1.0

- **Every panel reports last turn's actuals.** Financials, credit rating,
  listed share price and dividends show what happened, not a smoothed
  average, so a stalled sector reads as stalled and a good turn reads as a
  good turn.
- **Consumers entered the economy.** Households buy things, and how much they
  buy follows their jobs, their wages and prices. Give people work and rising
  pay and they spend more; let the cost of living climb and they pull back.
  That spending flows to the companies that sell to them, so where you build
  and who you employ shapes your own demand.
- **Player-run unions.** Every industry has a union you can organise. Fund a
  recruitment drive, run for union president, build a treasury, push for
  higher wages. Governments can outlaw unions by law, which freezes them until
  repeal.
- **Supply agreements are real deals**, with an agreed price and optional
  exclusivity, and they can cross borders.
- **Money behaves like money.** Savings pay the real rate rather than the
  headline rate, high-inflation currencies lose value, bond holdings have
  sensible position limits, and prices cannot fall into a runaway deflation
  loop.
- **Defense budgets buy from a real market**, at the era's exchange rate.
- **Buyouts settle cleanly.** Taking a company private past an index-fund
  holdout works, with the funds bought out at fair value, and index funds are
  paid correctly on any buyout, wind-down or nationalisation.
- **Command economies are in play.** The Soviet Union and East Germany run
  planned economies, with everything that implies for prices, currency and
  the central bank.

## A first look: sectors you build

This one is not live yet. It is a ground-up rework of how corporations
produce and sell, finished and sitting behind a switch that is off, and we
want you to see it before we decide anything, because it is the largest
economy change we have made.

Today, a sector is a number you buy more of. Under the new model, a sector is
a **factory you build**. You spend money on plant. It takes turns to come
online. It makes goods, up to the size of what you built. Those goods go to
market and some of them sell. What sold is your revenue, and revenue times
margin equals profit on every row, because there is only one revenue and you
can follow it from the plant to the ledger.

![The sector page under the new model: installed capacity, what the plant made today, and why the rest of it sat idle.](/changelog/plants/plant-panel.webp)

Why it matters:

- **You build the market.** Untapped demand is not a number in a table; it is
  unmet demand, measured in goods, shown to you as somewhere to build. Where
  players choose to put plant decides what each market looks like, which
  means more variance from world to world and less of the market arriving
  preset.
- **Your plant explains itself.** Each turn it tells you how much it made,
  how much sat idle, and what stopped it: short of energy, nobody buying, or
  a strike, each cause named with the units it cost you.
- **Costs are this turn's costs.** Inputs are billed at what they actually
  cost this turn, so when the price of energy moves, your profit moves and
  you can see the line that did it. A blackout or a port closure stops
  production. Idle plant still costs money, the way a real factory would.
- **Growth is an order, not a slider.** The build dialog shows what it costs
  and why, how long it takes, how many workers you will need, and how long it
  takes to pay for itself. It warns you when there is not enough demand in
  that market to sell what you are about to build.

![The build dialog, showing an itemised cost, construction time, extra staff needed, and how long the plant takes to pay for itself.](/changelog/plants/build-dialog.webp)

![The sectors list, with capacity, fill rate, revenue and profit for each holding.](/changelog/plants/sectors-tab.webp)

![Choosing where to build a first plant, with untapped demand shown per market.](/changelog/plants/first-build.webp)

The idea came from the advisors channel, from a player pressing on how sector
revenue relates to sector profit, and it grew into this. **None of it is
running in your game yet.** The switch stays off until we have run the
balance simulations, tested it in the sandbox, and had the advisors play it.
You will hear about it here first, and the numbers in these screenshots, like
the cost of a plant and how fast it pays back, are still being tuned.

## Governing

- **Impeachment and by-elections.** Presidents and governors can be impeached
  through the proper channels, and the vacancy is filled by a by-election.
- **Move to vacate the Speaker.** Any sitting member files a motion, the
  whole chamber votes, and an absolute majority removes the Speaker on the
  spot. The motion is chamber-wide, so the minority can force the question.
- **The Speaker's chair refills itself** if the Speaker loses their seat.
- **A Whip Room** on the congress leadership screen.
- **Chamber rules with teeth.** Cloture needs three fifths of the votes
  actually cast, state bills pass on a simple majority, and leadership
  positions reopen every cycle.
- **Electoral law is a channel you legislate in.** Franchise and voter
  registration access are set by law, so who may vote, and how hard it is to
  register, is a decision your legislature owns rather than a constant.
- **The Warsaw Pact states have the offices they really had.** A Chairman of
  the Council of State, a Chairman of the Presidential Council, a President
  of the State Council, a Chairman of the Presidium, each held automatically
  by the ruling party's chairman, the way the President of the PRC follows
  the General Secretary. Where a country has no such office, no office is
  shown.

## Since launch

1.0 was a start, not a stop. The systems below shipped in the weeks after

### The Supreme Court convenes

The United States has a Supreme Court, and it is yours to shape. Nine seats,
seeded for each era start with an authored roster of fictional justices; the
game invents its jurists the way it invents its politicians, so no real
judge's record is put in your hands.

Justices leave the bench on their own clocks. When a seat opens, the
President nominates, a player or a generated candidate, and the Senate
confirms, through the same kind of hearing window a cabinet pick gets. Fill
the bench with hawks on one axis and you have changed what the law can
become, because the docket is where it pays off.

Each era start carries an authored docket of landmark cases that come up for
decision in their real years.

When a case fires, the sitting court decides it. A court that still looks
like history rules the way history ruled. A court you have reshaped can rule
the other way, and a diverged ruling is enacted like legislation: it moves
the law, the law moves regional leans, and the shift lands on the nation's
positions timeline (here is [the American
one](/country/US/policy?view=record)) like any act of Congress. The Supreme
Court can move your electorate, which is exactly the reason presidents fight
over it.

Two deliberate limits. Race and equal-protection doctrine always resolves
the way it really did; Brown v. Board is not a coin your court gets to flip.
And alongside the authored docket, rare **surprise cases** spawn
procedurally, era-windowed and never real cases, so a long-running world's
court stays busy without inventing alternate history for actual litigants.

The court has its own page under the nation: composition and balance, the
justice roster with portraits, the nomination pipeline, and the docket's
history. Every ruling makes the news wire.

### Declaring war, and making peace

War entered the constitutional order.

- **Declaring war is a bill.** It needs two thirds of the chamber in every
  system of government, costs what a bill costs to propose, and carries a
  **war goal** from a fixed catalog: Conquest, Regime Change, Punitive, or
  Liberation. The goal is public, and it defines what winning means.
- **The executive holds the trigger.** Passing the bill authorises the war;
  the executive's declare-war control starts it.
- **You can sue for peace.** A peace offer is a real document with terms and
  an optional justification, open for 72 turns. The head of government or
  the foreign seat negotiates. An accepted deal stands both sides down.
- **Peace holds.** A concluded peace binds the pair under a 240-turn truce,
  so a war cannot be re-declared the moment the ink dries, and a fresh
  declaration against anyone has its own cooldown.

### Three more nations to govern

Finland, Austria and Greece are now full playable countries: governments,
legislatures, elections, cabinets, parties and budgets, not map decoration.
Austria brought its Bundesrat with it, and the engine learned to run
unicameral legislatures to seat these parliaments correctly. All three read
their eras: postwar Finland is paying off its war reparations to the Soviet
Union, and Greece in 1953 has no head-of-state office to leave vacant.

### The central bank gets a committee

Setting the interest rate stopped being one chair's private decision. Every
central bank now seats a seven-member rate-setting committee, styled on the
FOMC, with staggered terms and a published hawk-or-dove alignment for every
member.

The committee meets on a cadence. The chair tables a motion, hike, cut or
hold; generated members vote their alignment; player members get a
twenty-four-hour window to cast theirs. A motion passes only on a majority
of the full board, no-shows abstain, and turns never pause for a meeting.
Rate moves are capped per term, so a captured board can tilt policy but not
whipsaw it.

The seats themselves are political. The President nominates a player or a
technocrat to any seat, including the chair, and the Senate confirms.
Expired seats refill on their own, alignment flipped, so the board never
runs short and never calcifies. The bank's **FOMC tab** shows the roster,
the live meeting with its vote buttons, and the nomination form.

### Sectors

- **A new sector could earn nothing.** A sector founded after launch could
  build, staff, produce and sell while every money line read zero: a price
  was being derived from revenue the sector did not have yet, and the zero
  stuck. Prices now derive from what the sector makes. Any sector stuck at
  zero repairs itself on its next turn, and no capacity was lost.
- **Each output reports its own clear rate.** A sector that makes more than
  one thing shows which product cleared and which sat, instead of one blended
  figure. Input availability reads from the world market, a shortage slows a
  line but never cuts it below half, and the panel shows the rate your plants
  ran at.

### Command

- **Every conflict page tells you who you are in that war.** A panel names
  the seat you hold, Theater Commander, posted general, defense secretary, or
  none of them, what you may do at that front, and for everything you may
  not, who does it and where to find them. It also explains how troops reach
  a front: a unit is wherever its general is posted, and the Commanding
  General posts generals to conflicts. The same explanation sits on the
  defense secretary's Commands tab, where the chain starts.
- **Recent offensives name the outcome**, coloured from your own side's point
  of view whether the offensive was yours or the enemy's, and an unopposed
  advance is recorded as taken ground rather than leaving no trace.
- **New commands take fresh ids**, so creating a command in a later session
  cannot silently edit an older one. Any organisation already affected
  repairs itself on page load.
- **A rare double-battle is gone.** An offensive whose territorial update
  failed could be fought again the next turn, casualties and all. Resolution
  is now recorded safely.
- **Offensive odds stay on screen** after you commit, while you decide
  whether to hold or call it off, and battle reports say what the fighting
  did to the front: ground gained, ground lost, or the line held.

### Generals

- **Commanding a theater pays.** A Theater Commander earns half of what a
  formation at that front earned, for every battle fought in their theater,
  measured per formation so a superpower's commander does not outrun a small
  nation's for the same work. If they led units too, they are paid for both.
- **Every trait costs exactly one point**, promotions are worth four, and a
  commissioned general earns a point for every day of service, capped at
  twenty. An officer in a nation at peace still develops, and past the cap a
  general only grows by campaigning. A general who has reached Field Marshal
  keeps earning through war, up to fifteen more points. A maximal career buys
  55 of the tree's 115 traits, a peaceful one 24; everyone specialises.

### The Bank of England answers to the Chancellor

In every era start before 1997, the Bank of England has no operational
independence, because it did not have any. The rate is set by the head of
government or the Chancellor, the bank's page says so plainly, and there is
no policy committee, because the MPC was created by the very act that granted
independence.

Parliament can pass that act. An economy bill can carry a
**central-bank-independence** provision: grant it and rate-setting moves to
the Bank and its new committee; a post-1997 world can just as well revoke it
and take the rate back. The statute is yours to write, which is the point.
The calendar never flips it for you: a 1991 world that plays past 1997 keeps
Treasury control until its parliament decides otherwise. Scotland and Wales
share sterling and the Bank, so that decision belongs to Westminster alone,
the way an ECB member cannot legislate over a treaty institution.

### Central bank chairs come from nominations

The market pool is gone. A central bank chair used to be drawn 30% of the
time from the nation's richest players, top-three wealth guaranteed a spot on
the shortlist, and the "Potential Market Candidates" panel advertised it.
Being rich is not a monetary-policy qualification, so the whole channel is
removed: every chair now comes from the executive's nominations, lobbying
only weights candidates who were actually put forward, and with no nominees
the seat falls to a technocrat rather than to the wealth leaderboard.

### Elsewhere

- **Eastern-bloc planned economies have state plants in every sector.** A short
  "commanding heights" list left most industries with no state enterprise and
  an unreachable unowned pool (you cannot found private corps there). Every
  Eastern-bloc / Soviet sector now has a state enterprise with real plant
  capacity; the phantom unowned twins are gone.
- **A 1953 company is worth what it holds.** Share counts now scale with the
  era the way money does. A corporation founded in 1953 issues about 143,000
  shares rather than 10 million, so it opens at ten cents a share and a market
  value that matches the cash in its treasury. Before, the price hit the
  one-cent floor and the company opened worth roughly seven times what it
  actually held, which also meant anyone buying into its opening offer paid
  about seven times over. This covers every company the game creates:
  founded, state-spawned, spun off and privatised alike.
- **You can found a corporation in 1953 again.** Founding was rejecting every
  attempt in a 1953 world, whatever your wealth: the form correctly asked for
  the era's $14,333, and the server was still checking the modern $1,000,000
  behind it. The check now uses the era's own numbers, the ones you were
  shown. The cost summary also stops reporting a negative "extra treasury"
  when you found at the baseline.
- **The changelog stays up during maintenance.** A down-window is exactly
  when you come looking for what changed, so this page now stays reachable
  while the rest of the game is sealed.
- **Era-correct offices everywhere.** Greece's head of state is no longer
  reported vacant in 1953, an era where the office does not exist. Country
  pages read the era being played.
- **Tab strips scroll on phones**, so Foreign Affairs, Regime Health and six
  other off-screen tabs are reachable on the White House, Cabinet, Supreme
  Court, Political Operations, currency and portfolio pages.
- **Currency Exchange leads with the nations you can play.** The hero,
  main table and charts default to player-enabled currencies; everything else
  is one expand away, with market nations listed before planned economies.
- **Where a candidate stands now matters on two fronts.** A state's economic
  and social leans are genuinely independent, so the 1953 South reads as
  economically left and socially conservative at the same time. Social
  positioning now moves votes on its own instead of echoing the economic lean.
- **Under plants, you build or take over — you do not split unowned.** The
  first plant is one facility at an era-scaled entry fee, Build here opens the
  founding flow, and the old Split unowned controls are gone. Rival plant
  takeovers remain.
- **Build here keeps your state selected.** Opening Build here from a region
  board pins that state in the expand list instead of jumping to the biggest
  market. Founding and first-plant placement still work while turns are paused
  for registration or settling — only an admin corp-actions pause freezes
  existing CEOs.
- **Companies outside the United States can build again.** A British, Soviet,
  German, Chinese or Japanese company trying to open or split a sector was
  turned away with "invalid state ID", whichever region it picked — the server
  was checking the chosen region against a list of the fifty US states and
  nothing else, so no home region outside America could ever match. It now
  checks the region against your own country, which is what it meant to do all
  along. American companies are unaffected.
- **Staff can move you to your own country's regions.** Asking a moderator to
  change your home region only worked if you played the United States —
  everywhere else the request was refused, and the admin tools offered no
  region to pick in the first place. Moving a character to a different country
  failed for the same reason, on every country except the US. Both now work for
  every playable country.
- **The FOMC Board of Governors shows who sits there.** Technocrat members were
  rendering as Vacant even when seated; the roster now lists their names.

## Your defence budget is now an actual budget

The defence line in your national budget used to be decoration. Armies cost
nothing to keep, and a defence minister could buy divisions by borrowing against
the treasury without limit.

Now it is an account. It fills every turn from whatever your laws currently set
defence spending to — change the law and the new rate starts immediately, no
waiting for a fiscal year. Your standing army is paid for out of it, and new
units are bought from it.

A nation sitting at its historical starting force spends a little over half its
defence budget just sustaining that force. The rest is yours to build with.
Double the army and you are spending more than you take in.

**Running short has consequences, and they are reversible.** You can overdraw by
up to a year's funding — enough to fight a campaign — and that becomes national
debt. Past that, the troops you cannot pay start losing readiness, drifting down
toward a lower baseline instead of holding at their posture's. Fund them again
and they climb straight back. Nothing is ever lost permanently.

**Growing your economy now buys you a bigger army.** Previously it did not:
unit prices rose in lockstep with GDP, so a booming economy bought exactly the
same number of divisions. Prices now rise more slowly than the economy does, so
industrial growth is genuine military strength.

The recruitment screen shows the account, what it nets per turn after upkeep,
and — when you cannot afford something — how many turns until you can.

## Your defence industry starts arming your army

Until now, equipment was a number your units were stamped with and nothing could
ever change. A division raised in 1953 carried the same kit forever, and the
defence companies in your economy built weapons nobody bought.

**There is now a national arsenal**, held separately for each arm of service —
ground, naval, air, rocket, marine, space. Your own defence companies fill it
under contracts you award as defence minister, paid for out of the defence
appropriation, and new units draw from it when they are raised.

**A starved navy is now something you can see.** The arsenal shows what each arm
holds against what your current roster would need to be fully equipped, so
"the fleet is under-armed" stops being a feeling and becomes a number.

**An empty store never stops you recruiting.** A division raised with nothing in
the arsenal arrives hollow — full strength in men, no equipment — and fills up
over following turns as deliveries arrive. Scarcity costs you quality and time,
never the ability to act.

**Two different things now decide how good your equipment is.** How much arrives
is set by your industry's output — a bigger, better-run plant delivers more. How
*good* it is comes from that company's research. An advanced arms industry with no
production capacity gives you modern units with empty racks; mass production
without research gives you fully-equipped obsolete ones.

**For company owners:** defence plants pick what they build the same way they
always have, through their production strategy — and three new lines exist for
naval, missile and aerospace work. A plant under contract earns real revenue per
lot delivered, and researching further up the defence tree raises the quality it
is allowed to supply. Contracts are domestic only for now.

## Wars now cost equipment, not just lives

Fighting destroys materiel. A formation that comes out of a hard battle is not just
thinned and shaken — it has lost equipment, and that equipment has to be replaced
out of your national arsenal like any other.

That closes the loop: a war drains the arsenal, the arsenal is refilled by contracts
with your defence industry, and those contracts are paid out of the defence budget
your legislature votes. A long campaign is now a demand on your economy, not only on
your population.

**Armour protects your crews, not your tanks.** A heavy formation takes fewer
casualties than a light one — but a knocked-out vehicle is destroyed equipment
either way, so armour does not make a formation cheaper to re-equip.

**A beaten force is weaker next time.** Equipment feeds combat power, so a unit that
has been stripped and not yet re-equipped fights worse. Pulling a mauled formation
back to refit is now a real decision rather than a cosmetic one.
